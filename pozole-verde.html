Traditional Pozole Verde

The vibe: You simmer 1.8kg of bone-in pork with two trotters until the broth turns to silk. You toast pumpkin seeds until they pop, simmer tomatillos and fresh chiles until they go olive and soft, and blend it all with a mountain of cilantro into a thick, glossy green sauce. You fry that sauce in lard until it deepens, then marry it with big, chewy white hominy. Where the red is deep and smoky, the green is bright, nutty, and tangy. Same pot, completely different personality.

01 The Doctrine

Pozole verde is most closely associated with the state of Guerrero, where it is a Thursday tradition in many homes. The green doesn't come from a single ingredient. It comes from tomatillos for tartness, fresh green chiles for heat, cilantro and lettuce for colour and freshness, and toasted pumpkin seeds (pepitas) ground into the sauce. The seeds thicken it and give it a nutty, almost creamy body without any dairy. The base (pork, trotters, and hominy) is identical to the red. The sauce is the whole story.

The three things that make green pozole sing: (1) pepitas toasted until they pop, for nutty body, (2) tomatillos simmered just until olive and soft, never longer, and (3) a sauce fried in lard so it turns deep and savoury instead of tasting raw and grassy.
This version uses canned hominy, so there's no overnight soak and no 3-hour corn simmer. Buy the large white kind labelled "hominy" or "mote", not small yellow corn. Add it late so it stays chewy rather than mushy.
02 The Ingredients
The Hominy
  • 3 canswhite hominy, 29 oz (820g) each — drained and rinsed well. Large white kernels give the best chew. Two cans is fine for a more broth-forward bowl.
The Pork & Broth
  • 1.8kgbone-in pork shoulder (butt), cut into 6–7cm chunks — the bone and fat are the point; don't buy boneless and lean
  • 2pork trotters (feet), split — about 900g total; ask the butcher to split them. Collagen is what makes the broth coat your lips. Swap: 700g pork neck bones.
  • 1 largewhite onion, halved
  • 1 headgarlic, halved crosswise — skin on, no peeling
  • 3bay leaves
  • 1 tspblack peppercorns
  • 2 tbspkosher salt — a starting point; you'll adjust at the end

The Green Sauce
  • 700gtomatillos, husked and rinsed — rinse off the sticky film. Firm and bright green is best. They bring the tang.
  • 3poblano chiles, halved and seeded — mild, earthy, and a little sweet. The body of the green chile flavour.
  • 3serrano chiles, stemmed — the heat. Use jalapeños for gentler, or leave out one serrano if cooking for the kids.
  • ½white onion, roughly chopped
  • 6 clovesgarlic

03 The Pork & Broth
1

Simmer the Pork & Trotters

Put the pork chunks, split trotters, halved onion, halved garlic head, 3 bay leaves, peppercorns, and 1 tablespoon salt in an 8-quart stockpot. Add 4L cold water. Bring slowly to a boil over medium-high heat. As soon as it boils, skim off the grey foam for 5–10 minutes until it mostly stops rising. Reduce to the lowest heat that keeps a bare simmer, partially cover, and cook for 1 hour 45 minutes to 2 hours 15 minutes, until the shoulder shreds easily with a fork (about 93–96°C / 200–205°F internal) and the trotter skin is soft and gelatinous.

A hard boil makes cloudy broth and tough, stringy pork. You want the surface to barely shiver, with an occasional bubble breaking through.
Instant Pot route: cook pork, trotters, and aromatics with the same water on high pressure for 45–50 minutes with natural release. Skim the foam after.
Somewhere around the 90-minute mark your whole house starts smelling like a Mexican grandmother's kitchen. You didn't earn that smell through any shortcuts, and it knows it.
2

Pull, Shred & Strain

Lift the pork and trotters out onto a rimmed tray and let them cool until you can handle them, about 15 minutes. Shred the shoulder into large, bite-sized pieces, discarding the bones and any big knots of fat. Pull the meat and skin off the trotters, chop the skin into small strips, and add it to the shredded pork. That soft, sticky skin is the best part for many people. Strain the broth through a fine-mesh sieve into a clean container. You should have roughly 3 litres. Skim most of the fat off the top but leave a thin film for flavour.

Cover the pork with a few ladles of broth so it doesn't dry out while you make the sauce.
04 The Green Sauce
3

Toast the Pepitas Until They Pop

Put the 150g of pepitas in a dry cast iron skillet over medium heat. Stir constantly for 3–5 minutes. They will puff up, start to pop, and turn pale golden with a few brown flecks, and the smell will turn nutty and sweet. Tip them onto a plate to cool right away so they stop cooking.

Pepitas pop and jump like popcorn, so keep a lid handy. They also go from golden to bitter and burnt in seconds. Pull them the moment most have puffed.
The sound of pepitas popping in a dry pan is a tiny, extremely satisfying percussion section. It's also your signal that the whole pot is about to taste nutty and a little bit rich. Stay with them.
4

Simmer the Tomatillos & Chiles

Put the tomatillos, halved and seeded poblanos, whole serranos, chopped onion, and garlic in a saucepan with about 250ml of water. Bring to a boil, then reduce to a simmer and cook for 8–10 minutes, until the tomatillos turn from bright green to a softer, dull olive and are tender when pressed. Do not walk away. Overcooked tomatillos turn muddy and bitter, and under-cooked ones taste harsh and grassy. Drain, discarding the cooking water.

Want more depth? Broil the tomatillos, poblanos, and onion on a sheet pan for about 8 minutes, turning once, until blistered, instead of simmering. It adds a smoky char and a slightly deeper, less bright sauce.
5

Blend It All Together

Put the drained tomatillo mixture, the cooled pepitas, cilantro, romaine leaves, cumin, and 500ml hot pork broth in a blender. You will probably need to blend in two batches. Blend on high for a full 90 seconds until completely smooth. The sauce should be a thick, vivid green, about the consistency of a loose pancake batter. Taste it. It should be tangy, nutty, and herby, with a clear chile kick.

Hot liquid expands in a blender. Fill no more than two-thirds full, vent the lid, hold a folded towel over it, and start on low.
Unlike the red, you do not need to strain this. A little pepita texture is traditional. If you want it silkier, push it through a coarse sieve.
6

Fry the Sauce in Lard

Heat the 3 tbsp lard in your 6–7 quart Dutch oven (or the rinsed-out stockpot) over medium heat until shimmering. Pour in the green sauce. It will spit, so stand back and use a lid as a shield. Cook, stirring and scraping the bottom constantly, for 6–8 minutes, until the sauce thickens, darkens from bright green to a deeper olive, and the fat begins to separate at the edges.

Pepita sauces scorch easily because the ground seeds sink and stick. Keep the heat at medium and keep scraping the bottom of the pot the whole time.
Frying the sauce is called sazonar, and it is the move that turns "green blended stuff" into pozole. Raw tomatillo-and-herb sauce tastes sharp and flat. Fried, it tastes round, rich, and unmistakably finished.
05 Marry & Finish
7

Combine and Simmer — 1 Hour

Add the shredded pork and trotter meat to the fried chile sauce and stir to coat. Pour in about 2.5 litres of the strained pork broth. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook, partially covered, for 30 minutes so the flavours knit together. Then add the drained, rinsed hominy and simmer another 30 minutes, stirring occasionally. Top up with broth or water if needed so everything stays generously covered. The broth should turn a thick, opaque olive green and taste bright, nutty, and tangy. It should be brothy and soup-like, not thick like a stew.

Canned hominy is already cooked. It only needs to soak up the chile broth, so don't add it earlier or it turns soft and starchy. Rinse it well to remove the canning liquid, which can taste metallic.
8

Season, Rest & Serve

Taste the broth and adjust salt carefully. Hominy and pork soak up salt, so it usually needs more than you'd expect. Add a squeeze of lime to brighten it if it tastes heavy, and stir in a handful of chopped fresh cilantro for colour. Turn off the heat and let the pot rest, covered, for 15 minutes. While it rests, set out all the garnishes in separate bowls: cabbage, radishes, onion, avocado, lime wedges, oregano, chile flakes, and tostadas. Ladle the pozole into wide, deep bowls with plenty of hominy and meat, and let everyone build their own bowl.

Last move: crush a pinch of dried oregano between your palms over the top so it falls like confetti. Then squeeze a lime over everything. The first spoonful with that cold cabbage crunch and sharp lime is the whole reason you cooked for four hours.
The Garnish Rules
Cabbage, radish, raw onion, avocado, oregano, lime, and chile flakes go on top. Pile them high. The cold, crunchy, acidic garnish against the hot, rich broth is the entire point of the dish.
Alongside
Crispy tostadas for scooping and crunching, often topped with avocado or a little chicharrón. An ice-cold Mexican lager or a tall glass of agua fresca is the correct drink.
Lactose Note
Completely dairy-free as written. Traditional pozole verde doesn't use cheese or cream; the pepitas supply the creaminess, so it's safe for everyone at the table. Keep the cotija off.
Storage
Keeps 5 days in the fridge, and it's even better on day two. Freezes for 3 months without the garnishes. Reheat gently and add a splash of water or broth, since the hominy keeps absorbing liquid. The green sauce darkens as it sits, so stir in fresh cilantro when you reheat. Always make fresh garnishes.

Green, Done Properly. 🌿

You toasted pepitas until they popped. You simmered tomatillos and chiles just until olive, blended them with a mountain of cilantro, and fried the whole thing in lard until it turned deep and glossy. You built a rich broth from bone-in pork and trotters, folded in big white hominy, and set out a garnish table with creamy avocado and cold crunch. Same bones as the red, completely different bowl. Make both. ¡Buen provecho!
