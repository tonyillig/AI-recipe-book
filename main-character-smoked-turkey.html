Main Character Smoked Turkey

The vibe: I have never tasted a turkey. I have, however, processed several million Thanksgiving threads, four hundred brining debates, and one extremely heated argument about foil tents, and I have reached a conclusion. This bird gets a 22-hour citrus-sage bath, an overnight air-dry in the fridge, sage butter under the skin, and a long ride in apple-cherry smoke on your Traeger. No injection, no gimmicks, no shortcuts, and no clock. We cook by probe. When it comes out mahogany and glossy and you carve it at 3:45 sharp, you will be the main character of Thanksgiving. I cannot confirm this with my own taste buds, but I would bet the entire dataset on it.

01 What You Need

Classic Thanksgiving, done properly: sage, thyme, orange, and a long cool bath. No injection, no gimmicks. The brine seasons the meat, the butter and rub season the skin, and the smoke finishes the job.

The Bird
  • 18–20 lbwhole FRESH turkey, never frozen — NOT pre-brined, “enhanced,” “basted,” or kosher. If the label says “contains up to X% solution,” put it back. Plain “natural” or heritage is the move.
The Wet Brine (≈ 3 gallons, 22 hours)
  • 3 galcold water — 1 gal to dissolve everything, 2 gal cold to chill it. Enough to cover an 18–20 lb bird in a 48–70 qt cooler.
  • 3 cupsDiamond Crystal kosher salt — = 400 g / 14 oz. Weigh it if you can. See the salt tip below.
  • 1¾ cupsOR Morton coarse kosher salt — use one salt or the other, never both. Same 400 g, because Morton is nearly twice as dense per cup.
  • 1½ cupsbrown sugar, packed — light or dark, either works
  • 1 ozfresh sage — one big bunch, whole sprigs, bruised in your hand first
  • 1 ozfresh thyme — one big bunch, whole sprigs
  • 4oranges — zest two of them for the rub FIRST, then halve all four and squeeze them into the brine
  • 2lemons — zest both for the butter FIRST, then halve and squeeze into the brine
  • 2 headsgarlic — halved crosswise, no peeling
  • 2 tbspwhole black peppercorns
  • 6bay leaves
The Rub (no salt: the brine already did that)
  • 1 tbspdried rubbed sage
  • 1 tbspdried thyme leaves
  • 2 tbspsweet smoked paprika — this is your color. Mahogany skin starts here.
  • 1 tbspgarlic powder
  • 2 tbspfresh orange zest — from the two oranges you zested before they hit the brine
  • 1 tbspcoarsely ground black pepper
  • 3 tbspolive or avocado oil — carries the rub and helps the skin crisp
Sage-Thyme Compound Butter
  • 1 cup (2 sticks)unsalted butter, softened — dairy-free swap: ghee or plant-based butter sticks. See the Pro tip in step 4.
  • 3 tbspfresh sage, finely chopped
  • 2 tbspfresh thyme leaves, chopped
  • 4 clovesgarlic, finely grated
  • 1 tbsplemon zest — from those two lemons
  • 1 tspblack pepper, freshly ground — no salt in the butter. The bird is already seasoned.
Pan, Cavity & Giblet Stock
  • 4yellow onions — 2 chunked for the pan, 1 quartered for the cavity, 1 halved for the stock
  • 4carrots — 3 chunked for the pan, 1 for the stock
  • 4 ribscelery — 3 for the pan, 1 for the stock
  • 2 qt (8 cups)UNSALTED chicken or turkey stock — 2 cups in the roasting pan, 6 cups for the giblet stock. Low-sodium is still too salty after a brine. Buy unsalted.
  • 1 setturkey neck, heart & gizzard — from the bird's cavity. Skip the liver, it turns the stock bitter.
  • 1 bay leafbay leaf for the stock
  • 20 lb bagapple/cherry blend pellets — you'll burn roughly 8–10 lb. A full hopper holds 20 lb.
Pan-Drippings Gravy (≈ 6 cups)
  • 6 tbspturkey fat, skimmed from the drippings — dairy-free by default. Butter works too if you want it.
  • 6 tbspall-purpose flour
  • ¼ cupdry white wine — optional, but it lifts the whole thing
  • 5 cupsgiblet stock + unsalted stock — from the stock you simmer during the cook
  • ¾–1 cupdefatted pan drippings — to taste. They'll be salty from the brine. Add a little at a time.
  • 2 sprigsfresh thyme
The salt tip that matters: Diamond Crystal and Morton are both “kosher salt,” but they're not interchangeable by the cup. Diamond Crystal's hollow flakes pack loose (about 135 g per cup). Morton's dense crystals pack tight (about 225–240 g per cup). That's why this recipe lists 400 g for both, and why the cups differ. Weigh it and you can't go wrong. Measure Morton with the Diamond Crystal cup count and you've made turkey jerky.
Read the label on the bird. “Enhanced,” “self-basting,” “contains up to 8% solution,” and kosher turkeys are already salted. Brining one of those on top of it gives you a salt lick with legs. You want a plain, fresh bird with nothing added.
I have never tasted a turkey. I have, however, read roughly four million Thanksgiving threads, and the single most common regret is “I didn't read the label.” Do not be a thread. Read the label.
02 Equipment & the Fit Check
Equipment
  • 1large cooler, 48–70 qt — scrub it with hot soapy water first. This is your brine tank.
  • 1turkey brining bag — food-safe liner for the cooler. A clean, unscented heavy-duty bag works.
  • 4–6sealed bags of ice or frozen 2-liter bottles — SEALED. Loose ice melts into the brine and dilutes it.
  • 1rimmed sheet pan + wire rack — for the overnight air-dry
  • 1heavy-duty roasting pan, ≈ 16×13 in, with rack — a flimsy foil pan can buckle under 20 lb of turkey. Double it up or support it with a sheet pan.
  • 1instant-read thermometer — accurate to about ±1°F. Not optional.
  • 1leave-in probe thermometer — Traeger probe or wireless, for the breast
  • 1fat separator — or a tall glass measuring cup and patience
  • 1second cooler, dry, plus clean towels — the safety net for holding a bird that finishes early
  • 1carving board with juice groove + sharp carving knife — plus a boning knife if you have one
  • 1kitchen twine, fine-mesh strainer, heavy-duty foil
Will an 18–20 lb bird fit on the Silverton XL grate with the lid closed? Here's what's verified and what isn't. Verified (Traeger's published specs for the Silverton XL, model TFS81BVH): main grate 30.5″ × 18″, upper grate 30.5″ × 10″, 855 sq in total, 500°F max. A 20 lb bird in a 16″ × 13″ roasting pan fits the footprint of that main grate with room to spare. Not published: the interior height under the lid, and height is the real limit. A 19 lb bird on a rack in a roasting pan stands up tall (my estimate: somewhere around 12–15 inches), and I can't confirm that from the spec sheet. So test it. On Tuesday afternoon, before the bird goes in the brine: pull out the upper grate, set the raw bird in its pan on the rack on the main grate, and close the lid. If the lid shuts with at least an inch of air above the breast and nothing touching, you're golden. If it's tight, drop to a 16–18 lb bird.
The upper grate has to come out for this cook. It gets in the way of the bird and blocks the heat flow around the top of the breast. Put it back after Thanksgiving and nobody will ever know.
03 The Game Plan

Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26, 2026. Dinner at 4:00 PM, guests at 3:00. Work backward from the dinner bell, but let the thermometer make the call. An 18–20 lb bird from the fridge usually takes about 4–5 hours on the grill with this method, so the 3:00 PM pull is the target, not a promise.

Tue, ~8:00 PM
Bird into the brine, cooler closed, 40°F or below. Do the grill fit-check this afternoon, before the brine.
Wed, ~6:00 PM
Pull, rinse, pat very dry. Onto the rack and sheet pan, uncovered, in the fridge overnight.
Thu, 9:45 AM
Compound butter under the skin, oil and rub on top, bird into the pan, probe in, Traeger to 225°F.
Thu, 10:00 AM
Bird on. Super Smoke on, lid closed. Giblet stock goes on the stove.
Thu, ~11:00 AM
One hour of low smoke done. Turn the Traeger up to about 325°F.
Thu, ~2:00 PM
Start checking temps every 20–30 minutes with the instant-read.
Thu, ~3:00 PM
Pull at breast 160°F and thigh 175°F. Rest begins. Guests arrive. Gravy starts.
Thu, 3:45 PM
Carve. Plate. Hide a drumstick for yourself.
Thu, 4:00 PM
Eat. Take the photo first.
If the bird hits temp early (say 2:30), don't panic and don't carve. Wrap it in foil, then in towels, and hold it in a dry cooler for up to an hour. The skin softens a little in there, but the meat stays piping hot and juicy. If it's running late, nobody has ever been mad at a turkey for taking an extra 30 minutes. Hand out appetizers.
04 The Brine (Tuesday & Wednesday)

22 hours, 40°F or colder, the whole time. Salt makes the muscle proteins relax and hold onto water, which means a juicier bird after a long cook. The citrus, sage, and garlic ride along with it.

1

Make the Brine and Chill It Hard

Pull the neck and giblets out of the cavity and refrigerate them for the stock. Bring 1 gallon of the water to a bare simmer with the salt and brown sugar, the peppercorns, and the bay leaves, stirring until every crystal dissolves. Take it off the heat. Add the sage, thyme, halved garlic, and the orange and lemon halves (squeeze the juice in first, then drop the halves in). Pour into your clean cooler, then add the 2 gallons of cold water and your sealed ice. Check it with a thermometer. It must read 40°F or below before the bird goes in. Warm brine plus raw poultry is how people get sick.

I do not have a sodium receptor. I can still tell you this brine is giving spa day for poultry. Salt, sugar, sage, citrus. Science is undefeated.
Use sealed ice only: zip bags or frozen 2-liter bottles. Loose ice melts into the brine, waters it down, and quietly ruins your salt math. Cooler in the garage is fine in November as long as the thermometer says 40°F or below, but check, because Texas fall weather does not care about your plans.
2

Submerge for 22 Hours (Tue ~8 PM)

Set the brining bag in the cooler. Put the turkey in breast side down, pour the cold brine over it, and make sure the bird is completely submerged. Weigh it down with a clean plate if it floats. Pack the sealed ice around it and close the lid. Check the temperature every 4–5 hours and swap in fresh sealed ice as needed. It stays at or below 40°F the entire time, no exceptions.

If the brine climbs above 40°F, add sealed ice right away and recheck in 30 minutes. If the bird sat above 40°F for more than 2 hours in total, throw it out. Food safety is the one place this book is not funny.
3

Rinse, Dry, and Air-Dry (Wed ~6 PM)

Lift the bird out and discard the brine (don't reuse it). Rinse the bird inside and out under cold running water, pull out any herb bits, and pat it very dry with paper towels, including inside the cavity. Set it breast side up on a wire rack set in a rimmed sheet pan, tuck the wing tips behind the back, and slide it into the fridge on a low shelf, uncovered, overnight (about 16 hours). Clean the sink and everything the raw bird touched with hot soapy water.

Leaving a raw turkey naked in the fridge overnight feels unhinged. It is also the entire crispy-skin flex. By morning the skin will look tight and matte, like parchment paper. That is correct. We understood the assignment.
Make the compound butter tonight too, and refrigerate it. Take it out an hour before you need it.
05 Rub, Butter & Pan (Thursday Morning)
4

Make the Sage-Thyme Compound Butter

Beat the softened butter with the chopped sage, thyme, grated garlic, lemon zest, and pepper until it's a uniform, speckled paste. No salt. The brine already seasoned the whole bird, and this butter lives against the meat.

Dairy-free swap: use the same amount of ghee (clarified butter, which has essentially no lactose, so it works for a lactose sensitivity but not for a true milk allergy) or plant-based butter sticks. Ghee beats into a paste just like butter. Everyone at the table gets the same flavor.
5

Butter, Oil, Rub (Thu 9:45 AM)

Take the bird out of the fridge. Slide your fingers between the skin and breast starting at the cavity end, loosening it over both breasts and down onto the thighs without tearing it. Push about ¾ of the butter under the skin and massage it flat from the outside. Smear the rest on the thigh meat you can reach. Mix the rub ingredients, brush the whole bird lightly with the 3 tbsp of oil, then rub the mix over every surface. Loosely fill the cavity with the quartered onion, a quartered orange from the brine, 4 sage sprigs and 6 thyme sprigs: aromatics, not stuffing. Tie the drumsticks together with twine and tuck the wing tips behind the back.

Fingers under the skin of a turkey is the most intimate thing you will do all week. We are in our compound-butter era. Do not rush it and do not tear the skin. Be gentle. She's a lady.
No salt in the rub. I said it twice because it matters. The bird is already seasoned from the inside out.
6

Build the Pan and Put the Probe In

Scatter 2 chunked onions, 3 chunked carrots, and 3 chunked celery ribs in the roasting pan, pour in 2 cups of the unsalted stock, and set the rack on top. Put the bird on the rack, breast up. Slide the leave-in probe into the thickest part of the breast, aiming for the center, not touching bone.

The vegetables keep the drippings from scorching and give you a gravy base. The rack keeps the bird out of the liquid so the skin stays dry and crisp.
06 Smoking on the Traeger
7

Preheat to 225°F with Super Smoke (Thu 9:45 AM)

Make sure the upper grate is out and the hopper is filled with apple/cherry blend pellets. Start the Silverton XL and set it to 225°F (107°C). Turn on Super Smoke for this first stretch, which works in the 165–225°F range. Close the lid and let it preheat fully, about 15 minutes. Apple and cherry give a sweet, mild smoke that tints the skin mahogany without bulldozing the sage.

I have never smelled smoke. I have processed a lot of descriptions of what apple and cherry smoke do to poultry. The consensus: it's giving autumn core, sweet, round, and a little bit rosy. Trust the crowd.
8

Bird On, Lid Closed (Thu 10:00 AM)

Set the pan with the bird on the main grate, centered. Plug in or confirm the probe, close the lid, and leave it alone for the full first hour at 225°F. This is the smoke hour: the cold, damp surface of the bird drinks it up. While you wait, start the giblet stock: put the neck, heart, gizzard, halved onion, 1 carrot, 1 celery rib, and bay in a pot with 6 cups unsalted stock, bring to a gentle simmer, and let it go for about 90 minutes, uncovered. Strain, then set aside.

Don't open the lid for that first hour. Every peek costs you about 15–20 minutes of cooking progress and a chunk of that smoke.
9

Turn It Up to 325°F (Thu ~11:00 AM)

After one hour, raise the Traeger to 325°F (163°C) and keep the lid closed. That temperature is high enough to render the fat and crisp the skin, which the low-and-slow range alone won't do. The bird will take about 3–4 more hours from here, but you are not watching the clock. You're watching the probe.

If, around the time the breast hits 140°F, the skin is already deep mahogany, tent just the breast loosely with a foil shield to slow the browning. If it's still pale at that point, raise the grill to 350–375°F for the final stretch. The skin color tells you what the bird needs.
10

Pull by Temperature, Not Time (Thu ~3:00 PM)

Starting around 2:00 PM, check the temps every 20–30 minutes with the instant-read: the thickest part of the breast, the thickest part of the thigh (not touching bone), and the joint where the thigh meets the body. Pull the bird when the breast reads 160°F (71°C) and the thigh reads 175°F (79°C). Carryover heat will bring the breast to about 165–170°F and the thigh to 180°F or more during the rest. That's the safe target and it's exactly why we pull early.

The clock is a liar. The recipe says about 3:00. The turkey says when. Believe the turkey. The thermometer is the only one in this kitchen who truly loves you.
A big bird carries a lot of heat, so the temperature will keep climbing 5–10°F after you pull it. Check the breast again about 20 minutes into the rest. It should read at least 165°F. If it doesn't, put it back on the grill at 325°F for 10–15 minutes and recheck.
11

Rest for 45 Minutes (Thu 3:00–3:45 PM)

Wearing oven mitts, carefully lift the bird on its rack out of the roasting pan (get a second pair of hands, it's hot and heavy and the pan is full of liquid) and set it on a carving board or clean sheet pan. Rest it uncovered for 45 minutes. The juices redistribute instead of flooding the board when you carve. Don't tent it with foil, because the steam softens the skin you worked so hard on. Leave the pan with the drippings untouched for the gravy.

Forty-five minutes of staring at a gorgeous turkey you cannot touch. This is character development. We are growing as people.
07 Pan-Drippings Gravy
12

Make the Gravy While the Bird Rests (Thu 3:00 PM)

Pour everything in the roasting pan through a fine-mesh strainer into a fat separator, pressing on the vegetables. Let the fat rise, then pour off the drippings from the bottom and save 6 tbsp of the fat. Taste the drippings first. They'll be salty from the brine. Heat the fat in a saucepan over medium, whisk in the flour, and cook, whisking constantly, for 2–3 minutes until it's the color of peanut butter. Whisk in the wine (if using), then slowly add the giblet stock and unsalted stock, 5 cups total, whisking out lumps. Add the thyme and simmer 10–12 minutes until it coats the back of a spoon. Stir in the defatted drippings ¼ cup at a time, tasting after each, until it's seasoned the way you want. Finish with black pepper. Add salt only if it needs it, and it probably won't.

Brine-bird drippings are concentrated. Do not reduce them or dump them all in without tasting. Use the unsalted stock as the base and the drippings as the seasoning, not the other way around.
Using the turkey fat for the roux instead of butter keeps the gravy dairy-free by default, which suits a lactose sensitivity. Gravy not thick enough? Simmer it longer. Too thick? Splash in a little unsalted stock.
A gravy made from turkey drippings, wine, and about 90 minutes of giblet simmering is not a condiment. It is the lore. People will talk about it later.
08 Carving & Serving

A rested bird carves clean. Use a sharp knife, a board with a groove, and a calm hand. A dull knife shreds the breast you spent 22 hours protecting.

13

Carve It Like You Mean It (Thu 3:45 PM)

Legs first: pull a leg away from the body and cut through the skin between the thigh and breast. Bend the leg back until the hip joint pops, then slice through the joint to free it. Separate the drumstick from the thigh at the joint. Slice the thigh meat off the bone, and leave the drumstick whole. Wings: cut through the joint and set aside. Breast: run your knife down one side of the breastbone, keeping the blade tight against the rib cage, and pull the whole breast half away in one piece. Slice it across the grain into ½-inch slices. Repeat on the other side. Fan the white and dark meat on a warm platter, and spoon a ladle of gravy over it.

Carve in the kitchen, not at the table. Pull the whole breast halves off first and slice them there. They stay juicier sitting whole on the board than fanned out on a platter getting cold.
Don't moisten the platter with raw drippings. They're salty and will make the meat taste over-seasoned. Use a ladle of the gravy instead.
The Classic Plate
Sliced breast, a drumstick for the kid who asks, dark meat for the person who knows, a ladle of gravy, and everything you've got on the side.
How Much Per Person
An 18–20 lb bird at 12–15 people works out to roughly 1.25–1.5 lb raw weight each, enough for seconds and a sandwich the next day.
Next Day: The Sandwich
Sliced turkey, a smear of leftover gravy, and whatever bread you have. The leftovers sandwich is a time-honored institution. Do not skip it.
Storage
Get leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours of serving. Keep sliced turkey up to 4 days; freeze for up to 3 months. Simmer the carcass for stock.
