Traditional Pozole Rojo

The vibe: You simmer 1.8kg of bone-in pork with two trotters until the broth turns to silk. You coax big, chewy white hominy kernels until they burst open like little flowers. You toast dried chiles, blend them smooth, and fry that sauce in lard until it goes dark and glossy. Then you marry all of it in one pot and set out a table of crunchy, bright, acidic garnishes. This is the bowl people cook for birthdays, Christmas, and Mexican Independence Day. Slow, deep, generous, and worth every minute.

01 The Doctrine

Pozole (from the Nahuatl pozolli) is one of Mexico's oldest dishes, a stew built on hominy, which is dried corn treated with an alkali (nixtamalized) so the kernels swell, soften, and turn chewy. It comes in three classic colours: blanco (plain broth), verde (pumpkin seed and tomatillo, most associated with Guerrero), and rojo (dried red chiles, the one most people picture). This is rojo, built the traditional way: pork, trotters, real hominy, and a chile sauce that is strained and fried before it ever touches the pot. The garnishes aren't a suggestion. They are half the dish.

The three things that separate great pozole from a decent pork-and-corn soup: (1) trotters or bones for body in the broth, (2) a strained chile sauce fried in lard until it darkens, and (3) a fully loaded garnish table. Skip any of them and you'll notice.
Dried hominy (cacahuazintle, or any large white dried hominy) has the best chew and the most authentic flavour, but it needs an overnight soak and a 2–3 hour simmer. No time? Canned hominy works. See the swap note in the ingredients.
02 The Ingredients
The Hominy
  • 500gdried cacahuazintle or large white dried hominy — soaked overnight. Rancho Gordo-style hominy is already treated and needs no lime. Canned swap: 3 × 29 oz (820g) cans, drained and rinsed, added in the last 45 minutes.
  • ½white onion — for the hominy pot
  • 4 clovesgarlic — for the hominy pot
  • 1bay leaf
  • 3Lwater — for cooking the hominy, plus extra for topping up
The Pork & Broth
  • 1.8kgbone-in pork shoulder (butt), cut into 6–7cm chunks — the bone and fat are the point; don't buy boneless and lean
  • 2pork trotters (feet), split — about 900g total; ask the butcher to split them. Collagen is what makes the broth coat your lips. Swap: 700g pork neck bones.
  • 1 largewhite onion, halved
  • 1 headgarlic, halved crosswise — skin on, no peeling
  • 3bay leaves
  • 1 tspblack peppercorns
  • 2 tbspkosher salt — a starting point; you'll adjust at the end
  • 4Lcold water
The Red Chile Sauce
  • 8dried guajillo chiles — about 60g; stemmed and seeded. Bright, tangy, cranberry-red. The colour and the backbone of the sauce.
  • 3dried ancho chiles — stemmed and seeded. Dark fruit, raisin, gentle sweetness. Rounds off the guajillo.
  • 3chiles de árbol — optional; for heat. Leave them out if cooking for the kids.
  • ½white onion, roughly chopped — goes in the blender
  • 6 clovesgarlic
  • 1 tspground cumin
  • 1 tbspdried Mexican oregano — NOT Italian oregano; rub it between your palms to wake it up
  • 500mlhot pork broth — ladled from the pork pot, for blending

  • 6limes, cut into wedges

03 The Night Before & The Hominy
1

Soak the Hominy Overnight

Rinse the 500g of dried hominy in a colander, then cover it with at least three times its volume of cold water in a big bowl. Leave it on the counter (or in the fridge if your kitchen is warm) for 8–12 hours. It will roughly double in size. If you're using cacahuazintle, many cooks pinch off the little pointed tip of each kernel (the cabecita) after soaking. It's optional, but it helps the kernels flower evenly and is part of the tradition.

Soaking hominy is the culinary equivalent of setting an alarm for tomorrow: it asks nothing of you tonight and gives you everything back at dinner.
2

Cook the Hominy Until It Flowers

Drain and rinse the soaked hominy. Put it in a large pot with the ½ onion, 4 garlic cloves, 1 bay leaf, and 3L fresh water. Bring to a boil, skim any foam, then reduce to a gentle simmer, partially covered. Cook for 2–3 hours, topping up with hot water if the level drops below the corn. After the first hour, stir in 1 tablespoon of the kosher salt. The hominy is done when most of the kernels have burst open like little flowers (florecido) and the texture is tender but still pleasantly chewy, never mushy.

Run the hominy on the stove at the same time as the pork in Step 3 and the two finish together. Short on time? Use canned hominy and skip this step entirely. Rinse it well and add it at Step 9. You'll lose some chew and the flower effect, but it's a very respectable weeknight shortcut.
Instant Pot route: pressure-cook the soaked hominy with the aromatics and water for about 40 minutes with natural release, then check for flowering. Simmer it a bit longer on sauté if needed.
04 The Pork & Broth
3

Simmer the Pork & Trotters

Put the pork chunks, split trotters, halved onion, halved garlic head, 3 bay leaves, peppercorns, and 1 tablespoon salt in an 8-quart stockpot. Add 4L cold water. Bring slowly to a boil over medium-high heat. As soon as it boils, skim off the grey foam for 5–10 minutes until it mostly stops rising. Reduce to the lowest heat that keeps a bare simmer, partially cover, and cook for 1 hour 45 minutes to 2 hours 15 minutes, until the shoulder shreds easily with a fork (about 93–96°C / 200–205°F internal) and the trotter skin is soft and gelatinous.

A hard boil makes cloudy broth and tough, stringy pork. You want the surface to barely shiver, with an occasional bubble breaking through.
Instant Pot route: cook pork, trotters, and aromatics with the same water on high pressure for 45–50 minutes with natural release. Skim the foam after.
Somewhere around the 90-minute mark your whole house starts smelling like a Mexican grandmother's kitchen. You didn't earn that smell through any shortcuts, and it knows it.
4

Pull, Shred & Strain

Lift the pork and trotters out onto a rimmed tray and let them cool until you can handle them, about 15 minutes. Shred the shoulder into large, bite-sized pieces, discarding the bones and any big knots of fat. Pull the meat and skin off the trotters, chop the skin into small strips, and add it to the shredded pork. That soft, sticky skin is the best part for many people. Strain the broth through a fine-mesh sieve into a clean container. You should have roughly 3 litres. Skim most of the fat off the top but leave a thin film for flavour.

Cover the pork with a few ladles of broth so it doesn't dry out while you make the sauce.
05 The Red Chile Sauce
5

Stem, Seed & Toast the Chiles

Pull the stems off the guajillos, anchos, and árbol chiles. Slit each open and shake out the seeds. Toast in a dry cast iron skillet over medium heat for 20–30 seconds per side, pressing flat with a spatula, until they turn a shade darker, soften, and smell sweet and slightly smoky. Do not let them blacken.

Burnt chile is bitter, and the bitterness won't cook out. If any chile develops black patches or a harsh, acrid smell, toss it and toast a replacement.
6

Soak in Hot Water, Then Discard the Water

Put the toasted chiles in a heatproof bowl and cover with hot (not boiling) water. Weigh them down with a small plate so they stay submerged. Soak for 20 minutes until fully soft. Then drain and discard the soaking water. It carries bitterness and grit from the dried skins.

Yes, this is the opposite of what we did with the Texas Chili, where the soaking stock went straight into the pot. Different dish, different rules. Pozole wants a cleaner, brighter chile flavour, and the soak water is where the harsh stuff hides.
7

Blend & Strain Until Silky

Put the soaked chiles in a blender with the ½ onion, 6 garlic cloves, cumin, Mexican oregano, and 500ml hot pork broth. Blend on high for a full 90 seconds until completely smooth. Then push the sauce through a fine-mesh sieve with the back of a ladle into a bowl, scraping the underside of the sieve. Discard the tough skins that stay behind. You should have a deep, brick-red, pourable sauce, roughly the thickness of heavy cream.

Hot liquid expands in a blender. Fill no more than two-thirds full, vent the lid, hold a folded towel over it, and start on low.
Do not skip the straining. It is the single biggest difference between a rustic, slightly gritty pozole and one with a silky, restaurant-quality broth.
8

Fry the Sauce in Lard

Heat the 3 tbsp lard in your 6–7 quart Dutch oven (or the rinsed-out stockpot) over medium heat until shimmering. Pour in the strained chile sauce. It will spit, so stand back and use a lid as a shield. Cook, stirring and scraping constantly, for 6–8 minutes, until the sauce darkens from bright red to deep brick, thickens into a paste-like consistency, and the lard starts to separate and pool at the edges.

This step is called sazonar or "seasoning" the sauce, and it is the move that makes the whole pot taste like it came from someone's abuela instead of a recipe. Raw chile sauce tastes sharp and flat. Fried chile sauce tastes rich, round, and finished.
06 Marry & Finish
9

Combine and Simmer — 45 Minutes

Add the drained hominy and the shredded pork and trotter meat to the fried chile sauce and stir to coat. Pour in about 2.5 litres of the strained pork broth. Add more water if needed so everything is generously covered. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook, partially covered, for 45 minutes to 1 hour, stirring occasionally, so the flavours knit together. The broth should look like a deep red-orange and taste rich and layered. It should be brothy and soup-like, not thick like a stew.

Using canned hominy? Add it here and simmer for only the last 30 minutes. It's already cooked, so more time just makes it mushy.
10

Season, Rest & Serve

Taste the broth and adjust salt carefully. Hominy and pork soak up a lot, so it usually needs more than you'd expect. Add a squeeze of lime to brighten it if it tastes heavy. Turn off the heat and let the pot rest, covered, for 15 minutes. While it rests, set out all the garnishes in separate bowls: cabbage, radishes, onion, lime wedges, oregano, chile flakes, and tostadas. Ladle the pozole into wide, deep bowls with plenty of hominy and meat, and let everyone build their own bowl.

Last move: crush a pinch of dried oregano between your palms over the top so it falls like confetti. Then squeeze a lime over everything. The first spoonful with that cold cabbage crunch and sharp lime is the whole reason you cooked for four hours.
The Garnish Rules
Cabbage, radish, raw onion, oregano, lime, and chile flakes go on top. Pile them high. The cold, crunchy, acidic garnish against the hot, rich broth is the entire point of the dish.
Alongside
Crispy tostadas for scooping and crunching, often topped with avocado or a little chicharrón. An ice-cold Mexican lager or a tall glass of agua fresca is the correct drink.
Lactose Note
Completely dairy-free as written. Traditional pozole rojo doesn't use cheese or cream, so it's safe for everyone at the table. Keep the cotija off.
Storage
Keeps 5 days in the fridge, and it's even better on day two. Freezes for 3 months without the garnishes. Reheat gently and add a splash of water or broth, since the hominy keeps absorbing liquid. Always make fresh garnishes.

Pozole, Done Properly. 🌶️

You soaked the hominy overnight and simmered it until it flowered. You built a rich broth from bone-in pork and trotters. You toasted, soaked, blended, strained, and fried a red chile sauce the way it has been done for generations. Then you set out a garnish table that made the whole thing sing. This is the pot people remember from the best celebrations of their lives, and now it's in your recipe book. ¡Buen provecho!
